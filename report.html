2. Game

Name and type

The game is called MindMatch. It is a card-matching memory game. A grid of cards starts face down. The player turns over two cards at a time and tries to find every matching pair using the fewest moves. The game tests short-term visual memory: the player has to remember where each picture was after it flips back over.

It meets the three game requirements:

  • Tests memory: the player recalls card positions across turns.
  • Levels: Beginner (12 cards), Skilled (16 cards), and Expert (20 cards).
  • Configuration: three card themes, Animals, Fruits, and Space.

Inspiration sites

I looked at the three example sites from the assignment. Here is what each one contributed.

helpfulgames.com memory grid The core flip-and-match grid came from here. I kept the simple grid idea but replaced the plain blue tiles with a clear theme, larger cards, and visible progress, since the original gives almost no feedback.
memorylosstest.com pair finding This site frames memory practice around finding pairs and tracking results. It pushed me to add a score summary and a personal-best record so a returning player can see progress, which Robert's persona needs.
mathsisfun.com Simon Says The Simon Says game showed how a level setting changes challenge in a memory game. It is the reason MindMatch has three difficulty levels that grow the board rather than a single fixed size. I did not copy its sequence mechanic.
The interfaces of all three examples are dated and give weak feedback. MindMatch keeps the proven pair-matching idea and rebuilds the interface with a consistent color theme, animation, and live stats.

Storyboard A . Maya . warm and playful, mobile

Theme: purple and pink, rounded phone cards, large tap targets. Interaction: tap a card to flip it.

Step 1, set up: a phone screen with pill buttons for Easy / Medium / Hard and theme pills (Animals, Fruits, Space), then a bright Start button.
Step 2, play with feedback: a compact grid, a top row showing pairs, moves, and time, and a cheerful toast message when a pair matches.
Step 3, win: a celebration screen with a star rating, final moves and time, and a New best score line.

Storyboard B . Robert . calm and clear, desktop

Theme: teal on a light background, wide desktop window, large square cards, a side panel with guidance and progress. Interaction: click or keyboard.

Step 1, set up: a browser window with Beginner / Skilled / Expert and theme choices on the left, and a How it works help box on the right.
Step 2, play with feedback: a larger board beside a stats panel with a progress bar and a plain sentence describing what just happened.
Step 3, session summary: a summary with moves, time, best moves, and a trend line comparing today to the last session.
The two storyboards differ on purpose: different color themes, different device layouts (vertical phone vs wide desktop), different typography (rounded sans vs serif), and different feedback styles (playful toast vs calm status panel).

Visual design choices

Color themeThe portfolio's dark navy with amber and blue accents. Amber marks the main action, blue and green mark stats and matched cards. One consistent palette across every screen.
TypographyA large bold title with a two-tone "Mind" plus amber "Match" draws the eye first. Stat numbers are large and bold, labels are small and quiet, which sets a clear hierarchy.
Layout and negative spaceA centered single column with generous spacing keeps focus on the board. The setup, game, and win screens each show one task at a time so nothing competes for attention.
Contrast, scale, balanceLight card faces sit on a dark board for strong figure-ground contrast. The board is the largest element, the stat row is secondary, and actions sit below in balance.

Gestalt principles applied

  • Similarity: all face-down cards share one look, so they read as a single set of choices. Matched cards switch to a green outline, grouping them as solved.
  • Proximity: even gaps in the grid group the cards into one board, separate from the stats above.
  • Figure and ground: bright card faces stand out clearly against the dark background.
  • Common region: the stat row and each card sit in their own bordered region, which tells the eye what belongs together.
  • Closure: the progress count and grid let the player sense the whole game even while most cards are still hidden.

Relationship to the storyboards

The final design is a combination of both storyboards. The screen flow (set up, play with feedback, end summary) and the live stat row come from both. The theme picker and animated celebration come from Maya's storyboard. The difficulty levels and saved best score come from Robert's storyboard. The color theme follows the existing portfolio rather than either storyboard palette, so the game feels like part of the same site.
